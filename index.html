Why this matters

With Everest, more than 5 random starts or smaller learning steps take far too much compute. With our surrogate, we can start from an almost unlimited number of random controls, with much smaller learning steps.

It also lets us go beyond NPV. Conventional solvers are too slow for this. We can keep NPV at its max and also:

  • delay water breakthrough
  • cut gas injected and gas produced
  • cut water injected and water produced
  • use less energy

So we searched much more of the control space

We ran 100 rounds instead of 5, in only 48 seconds. The same 100 rounds in Everest would take:

48 secondsOur surrogate, 100 rounds
120 hoursEverest, with its larger steps (26x larger, which is not ideal)
3,120 hoursEverest, with the same small steps we used

Where 3,120 hours comes from: small steps follow the NPV surface more closely, so they are less likely to jump over a better plan. But each step moves less, so a round needs about 26x more steps to cover the same ground. One Everest round then takes about 26 × 1.2 = 31.2 hours, and 100 rounds take 100 × 31.2 = 3,120 hours. That is about 130 days of nonstop computing.

Training cost: 11 hours on only 1 CPU core, paid once upfront

This one-time compute built the surrogate, and it covers all the results on this page. Even with this cost included, we are faster: 11 hours plus 48 seconds of optimization, against 120 hours for 100 Everest rounds with large steps.

And we can keep using the surrogate with no more training, as long as the history-matched model and the field structure do not change much.

Same max NPV, better everything else

We searched the control space widely. We found 5 other control plans with the same max NPV (within 0.5%; three of them are even higher). These plans:

  • produce 6–11% less gas and inject 5–10% less gas
  • produce 2–5% less water and inject 2–6% less water
  • use 4–9% less energy
  • delay field water breakthrough from month 1 to month 28–32 (in four of the five plans)
